## Learning outcomes

Upon completion of the course, students are expected to possess a solid understanding of the fundamental principles of Materials Science and Technology and Applied Chemistry, comprehending the relationships between atomic structure, defects, diffusion, phase diagrams, and the mechanical, electrical, and thermal properties of the main engineering material classes. Students will be able to apply these concepts to evaluate manufacturing processes and heat treatments suitable for optimizing component performance, defining the necessary testing and quality control procedures to ensure compliance with design specifications. Furthermore, students will master basic concepts of water chemistry, including contaminants and treatment methods, as well as the production technology and performance of inorganic binders. Ultimately, students will develop the critical judgment required to select appropriate materials and mitigate degradation phenomena in industrial environments, communicating effectively using proper technical language and demonstrating independence in consulting advanced technical literature and engineering standards

## Learning objectives

The course aims to provide fundamental knowledge in Materials Science and Technology as well as Applied Chemistry, enabling students to understand the relationships between the structure of matter (from atomic to macro-scale), properties, processing technologies, and operational performance.

The course contributes to the educational goals of the Degree Course in Industrial Engineering by providing:

- In-depth knowledge of the structure, as well as the mechanical, physical, and chemical properties of the main classes of engineering materials (metals and alloys, polymers, ceramics, semiconductors, and composites), along with primary industrial production, processing, and heat treatment technologies.
- The ability to apply this knowledge to develop sound criteria for material selection and manufacturing process specification during engineering design.
- Analytical skills to identify and solve engineering problems related to material degradation, fracture mechanisms, durability, and lifetime optimization of industrial components and structures.
- Fundamental knowledge of applied chemistry focusing on water chemistry (physicochemical characteristics, hardness, organic and inorganic contaminants, and water treatments) and the technology of inorganic binders (aerial lime, gypsum, cements, and concretes), correlating their chemical composition with performance and durability.

## Prerequisites

Successful completion of the course requires a solid foundation in General Chemistry (atomic structure, chemical bonding, reactivity, and solutions), General Physics (classical mechanics and electromagnetism), and Mathematical Analysis (differential and integral calculus).

## Contents

Module 1: Materials Science and Technology (9 ECTS / 90 hours)

Atomic Structure, Defects, and Solid-State Diffusion: Review of atomic bonding, crystalline and amorphous structures. Crystallography (unit cells, Miller indices, Bravais lattices, interstitial sites) and allotropy. Crystal structures of metals, ceramics, and polymers. Lattice defects (point, line/dislocations and Schmid's law, surface, volume). Solid-state diffusion, Fick's laws, and activation energy.

Mechanical Properties and Testing: Stress and strain concepts. Tensile, compressive, flexural, and hardness tests (Brinell, Rockwell, Vickers, Knoop). Impact testing and ductile-to-brittle transition. Fracture mechanics, fracture toughness, fatigue, and creep behavior.

Phase Diagrams and Phase Transformations: Solid solutions. Binary equilibrium phase diagrams (eutectic, eutectoid, peritectic). $Fe-Fe3C phase diagram. Nucleation and grain growth. Transformation kinetics, TTT/CCT diagrams, and martensitic transformations.

Heat Treatments and Strengthening Mechanisms: Solid solution strengthening and strain hardening. Conventional heat treatments of steels (annealing, normalizing, quenching, tempering, hardening, isothermal and surface treatments). Precipitation hardening and aging.

Engineering Alloys and Processing Technologies: Foundry processes, hot/cold plastic deformation, welding, and powder metallurgy. Designation, properties, and applications of ferrous (carbon, alloy, stainless steels, cast irons) and non-ferrous alloys (Al, Mg, Cu, Ni alloys).

Ceramic and Polymeric Materials: Traditional and advanced ceramics, glasses, glass-ceramics, refractories, and processing methods. Thermoplastics, thermosets, and elastomers: polymerization, structure, glass transition temperature ($T_g$), properties, and processing.

Physical Properties of Materials: Electrical properties (band theory, semiconductors, dielectrics). Thermal properties (heat capacity, thermal expansion, conductivity). Magnetic properties (diamagnetism, paramagnetism, ferromagnetism, soft/hard magnetic materials) and introduction to superconductivity.

Module 2: Applied Chemistry (3 ECTS / 30 hours)

Water Chemistry and Treatment Technologies: Physicochemical properties of water, hydrologic cycle, and natural waters. Water hardness. Organic and inorganic contaminants. Water purification, softening, demineralization, and industrial water conditioning.

Inorganic Binders Technology: Definition and classification of binders. Aerial binders (gypsum, aerial lime) and hydraulic binders (hydraulic lime, Portland cement, blended cements). Setting and hardening reactions, microstructure of hydrated cement paste, and concrete durability.

## Teaching methods

The course is structured into the following instructional activities:

Lectures: Comprehensive presentation of theoretical concepts in Materials Science, Technology, and Applied Chemistry, supported by multimedia slides and board demonstrations.

Guided Classroom Exercises: Resolution of numerical and conceptual problems regarding crystallography, binary phase diagram interpretation, mechanical properties, and water chemistry calculations.

Case Studies and Practical Examples: Guided discussions of real-world examples of material failure, degradation, or selection, aimed at contextualizing fundamental theoretical principles within engineering applications and supporting the learning and understanding of core concepts.

Industrial Field Trips: Guided visits to industrial facilities (such as cement plants or water treatment sites) to observe large-scale manufacturing processes of inorganic binders and industrial water systems firsthand.

## Assessment methods

Student learning is evaluated through an **individual oral examination**. The exam assesses theoretical knowledge and understanding of Materials Science, Technology, and Applied Chemistry, as well as the student's ability to link structure, properties, and processing, and their use of proper engineering terminology.

The final grade will reflect the following levels of preparation:

Sufficient (18–20/30): Basic and essential understanding of material structure and properties across main material classes, as well as fundamental concepts of water chemistry and inorganic binders.

Adequate (21–23/30): Fair ability to correlate structure, properties, manufacturing processes, and material performance, alongside a adequate understanding of water and binder technologies.

Good (24–26/30): Solid knowledge of all course topics, demonstrating clear analytical skills in material selection, structural design, processing, and applied chemistry systems.

Outstanding (27–30 cum laude): Comprehensive and deep mastery of the entire syllabus, superior critical thinking skills regarding material durability, lifetime optimization, and case study analysis, combined with flawless technical communication
