## Learning outcomes

Upon successful completion of this course, students will be able to:

1. Understand the role of various corporate functions and their interdependencies, as well as their interactions with external nodes within the supply chain in which the focal firm operates;
2. Analyze and interpret the architecture of technical-economic supply chains, demonstrating conceptual and operational mastery of core supply chain processes;
3. Design and critically evaluate alternative supply chain configurations;
4. Understand and apply decision-support tools typical of supply chain management (e.g., supplier selection and evaluation, make-or-buy decisions).

## Learning objectives

This course aims to provide students with the fundamental competencies required to understand the structural, organizational, and managerial logic of industrial production for goods and services. The discussion focuses on the configuration and operation of supply chains, examining the interdependencies among the nodes (suppliers, distributors, customers) of the value system in which the focal firm's value chain is embedded.

This course contributes to achieving the general educational objectives of the Bachelor's Degree in Industrial Engineering (Management Engineering Track) by promoting, primarily but not exclusively, the development of Knowledge and Understanding regarding:

- The operational modes of complex systems, such as logistics, production, and organizational systems;
- Quantitative approaches combined with an attention to factors characterized by low determinism and predictability, typical of the organizational systems in which future engineers will operate;
- Specific areas of action, including procurement and materials management, industrial and production organization, as well as the organization and automation of production systems.

Regarding the ability to apply the acquired knowledge and understanding, the course enables students to:

- Identify, formulate, and solve management engineering problems using fundamental and established methods;
- Analyze products for industrial applications, as well as management engineering processes and methods;
- Select and appropriately apply the analytical and modeling methods covered in class;
- Independently explore specific engineering topics relevant to management engineering, both in advanced master-level studies and in the professional workforce;
- Master and understand the terminology used in management engineering and the broader technical-engineering domain, in order to justify, support, and argue their technical, technological, organizational, and managerial decisions;
- Manage and control production and organizational processes within a context where economic and financial aspects—alongside technical and technological ones—are of growing importance;
- Operate effectively in scenarios where technical and technological issues are intertwined with economic, financial, and organizational factors, maintaining a holistic perspective that ensures alignment between technological choices, corporate strategies, and industry-specific requirements.

## Prerequisites

- Fundamentals of Engineering Economics (with a particular focus on cost structures and the production function);
- Fundamentals of Mathematical Analysis.

## Contents

In the introductory section, after defining the concept of the focal firm, the course highlights the characteristics of goods and services, emphasizing the current trend toward servitization.

The course then examines the value chain activities of the focal firm, its main organizational functions, and its interactions with other actors within the broader value system.

Subsequent modules address industrial costs and economies of scale, volume, scope, and breadth.

A specific module is dedicated to the concept of process and system optimization, emphasizing the necessity of trade-offs in decision-making.

Considerable attention is given to demand forecasting across different planning horizons (long, medium, and short term; e.g., capacity creation vs. capacity adjustment, master planning, and production scheduling).

The course then moves into the detailed analysis of supply chain processes, starting with manufacturing processes and their impact on production costs. This section includes evaluations regarding outsourcing decisions.

An introductory overview of logistics processes is provided, with particular emphasis on inventory and warehouse management.

This is followed by a focus on procurement processes and their internationalization.

Through a dedicated seminar, the importance of the circular economy in supply chains is explored.

The course concludes with the fundamentals of supply chain risk management and the evaluation of supply chain resilience.

## Teaching methods

The teaching methods adopted in this course (and the corresponding learning activities required of students) include:

- Lectures, aimed at acquiring foundational knowledge and understanding;
- Interactive learning, through the presentation of real-world problems and decision-making scenarios, aimed at developing problem-solving skills and identifying appropriate solutions (case studies, incident cases);
- Guided practical sessions (tutorials), designed to enhance communication skills and the ability to articulate and defend technical arguments.

The use of Artificial Intelligence (AI) tools is not foreseen for any teaching activities within this course.

The instructors strongly discourage the use of AI tools for:

- a. Drafting or revising lecture notes;
- b. Seeking explanations regarding course topics.


In any case, students who choose to use AI tools—for any purpose whatsoever—will be held solely responsible for any inaccuracies, incorrect information, or errors in their academic work and responses.

## Assessment methods

There is no formal prerequisite testing. During the first lecture, the instructors will highlight the foundational knowledge deemed essential for the course (as outlined in the *Prerequisites* section). Students are strongly encouraged to promptly bridge any knowledge gaps.

Active student participation is fostered throughout the course using the aforementioned teaching methods, with a focus on engaging students in logical reasoning during lectures. This allows instructors to continuously gauge learning progress by asking targeted questions and prompting discussion. The pace of lecture topics will be adjusted according to student-instructor interaction.

In addition, learning progress is monitored through voluntary midterm examinations (partial tests), detailed below.

The final examination consists of:

1. Written Exam: Comprising 4 numerical exercises based on the techniques presented during the course. Passing the written portion (grade $\ge 18/30$) is required to proceed to the oral exam. The written exam accounts for 60% of the final grade.
2. Oral Exam: Consisting of two questions, which may cover previous test topics and the discussion of student work. Passing the oral exam (grade $\ge 18/30$) accounts for 40% of the final grade.

Students may be exempt from the written final exam by passing two midterm tests held during the semester. Each midterm test consists of two exercises of a quantitative nature (e.g., numerical problems) and/or qualitative nature (e.g., discussion of a business strategy based on a case study).

- If a student fails at most one midterm test, they may make it up during an official exam session by retaking only the section of the written exam corresponding to that specific test.
- Students who successfully pass both midterm tests (or make up the failed one) can proceed directly to the oral exam during an official exam session.
- The oral exam must be taken in the same exam session in which the written portion is passed or the failed midterm is successfully made up.
