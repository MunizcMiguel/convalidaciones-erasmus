## Contents

The syllabus of this 6-ECTS course covers the following topics: Mathematical preliminaries (0.5 ECTS) Vector calculus and applied vectors; geometry of areas. Kinematics (1.0 ECTS) Kinematics of the point; rigid-body motion and particular rigid motions; displacements; constraints; settlements; degrees of freedom and degrees of lability. Statics (1.0 ECTS) Equations of statics and equilibrium conditions; free-body diagrams and constraint reactions; graphic statics; static–kinematic duality; principle of virtual work for rigid bodies. One-dimensional elastic beams (1.0 ECTS) Kinematics; internal force characteristics; constitutive law and elastic problem of the beam; identity of virtual works; trusses. Dynamics (0.5 ECTS) Review of Newtonian dynamics and outline of Lagrangian dynamics; applications to the rigid body and to one-dimensional systems. Mechanics of materials (1.0 ECTS) Outline of stress and strain analysis; mechanical properties of materials (elasticity, plasticity, viscoelasticity); failure modes (brittle and ductile fracture, viscoelastic failure, fatigue and creep); outline of the phenomenon of cellular mechanotransduction.

## Learning objectives

The course introduces the student to the use of simplified structural models, based on the mechanics of rigid bodies, for the analysis of the main physical, kinematic and dynamic models of the human body. The objective is to provide tools to understand the mechanics of movement, to interpret biomechanical models and to apply them in relevant contexts. D1. Knowledge and understanding By the end of the course, the student will know and understand: the basic mathematical tools (vector calculus, applied vectors, geometry of areas); the kinematics and statics of systems of rigid bodies; the elastic problem of the one-dimensional beam; the fundamentals of Newtonian dynamics, with an outline of Lagrangian dynamics; the link between structure, function and mechanical properties of materials (elasticity, plasticity, viscoelasticity), with particular attention to biological tissues; the main failure modes of materials and the mechanisms governing the interaction between mechanical loadings and material response, including an outline of cellular mechanotransduction. D2. Applying knowledge and understanding The student will be able to: describe and solve simple equilibrium problems of statically determinate or under-constrained systems, in particular in the biomechanical field; tackle problems concerning the equilibrium, kinematics and dynamics of the human body, also by means of structural analysis tools such as the Principle of Virtual Work; draw free-body diagrams and determine constraint reactions and internal forces; analyse simple trusses. D3. Making judgements The student will be able to autonomously select the solution method best suited to the problem at hand, to evaluate the adequacy of the adopted biomechanical model and to judge the reliability and physical consistency of the results obtained. D4. Communication skills The student will be able to present the methodologies adopted and the results obtained in a clear and conscious manner, with appropriate technical language, also by means of graphical works. D5. Learning skills The student will develop the aptitude to autonomously search for solutions to problems not previously addressed or of an innovative nature, a necessary prerequisite for the subsequent courses and for future professional development. The minimum learning level is considered achieved when the student demonstrates knowledge and understanding adequate to describe and solve simple equilibrium problems of statically determinate or under-constrained systems, in particular in the biomechanical field.

## Assessment methods

The exam consists of an oral test organised in two steps: in the first part, the student is asked to solve exercises on the topics covered in class; in the second part, these are discussed and some themes addressed during the course are examined in depth, in order to also assess the student&#39;s communication and learning skills. The exam is aimed at ascertaining: the knowledge and understanding of the course contents (D1); the ability to apply such knowledge to the solution of exercises and simple biomechanical problems (D2); the independence of judgement in the choice of the solution method and in the interpretation of the results (D3); the command of technical language and the clarity of exposition (D4); the learning skills and the ability of autonomous in-depth study (D5). The final mark, expressed on a scale of thirty (out of 30), takes into account, in an overall manner, the knowledge acquired, the ability to apply it to concrete problems, as well as the communication and critical skills shown during the exam. The minimum learning level (18/30) is considered achieved when the student demonstrates knowledge and understanding adequate to describe and solve simple equilibrium problems of statically determinate or under-constrained systems, in particular in the biomechanical field. The maximum mark, possibly with honours (cum laude), is awarded to students who demonstrate full command of the course contents, methodological rigour and command of language. Students with certified SLD, disabilities, or special educational needs should contact the professor and department coordinator early in the course to arrange suitable exam accommodations.
