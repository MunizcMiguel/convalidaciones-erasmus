## Contents

Fundamentals of electromagnetics - Electric and magnetic fields - Electromagnetic waves - Maxwell’s equations - Field/matter interaction Electromagnetic field behavior as a function of wavelength - Quasi-static regime (λ ≫ body size) - Intermediate regime (λ ~ body size) - Optical regime (λ ≪ body size) Electromagnetic properties of biological tissues - Conductivity, permittivity, permeability - Conduction and displacement currents - Polarization and dipoles Electromagnetic dosimetry - SAR (Specific Absorption Rate) - Human body models - Numerical methods: FDTD, FEM, impedance methods Biomedical applications of electromagnetic fields - Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) - Electrical stimulation (pacemakers, TENS) - Hyperthermia for cancer therapy - Microwave and optical imaging - Communication and powering of implantable devices Regulatory and safety aspects - International guidelines - Exposure limits and regulations Laboratories and exercises - Electromagnetic field visualization - Computational simulations - Environmental measurements

## Learning objectives

The course aims to provide an intuitive and operational understanding of electric and magnetic fields and of their interaction with biological tissues. Students will acquire the fundamental principles of electromagnetism and understand the role of electromagnetic phenomena in several biomedical applications, such as magnetic resonance imaging, electrical and magnetic stimulation, hyperthermia therapy, and technologies for diagnosis and treatment. By the end of the course, students will be able to describe and interpret the main electromagnetic phenomena relevant to biological systems, understand the basic physical models of field–tissue interaction, and critically analyze the assumptions, limitations, and domains of validity of such models. They will also be able to apply the acquired knowledge to the understanding of biomedical devices and techniques based on electromagnetic fields, assessing their operating principles, performance, and practical implications from both qualitative and quantitative perspectives. The course also aims to develop the ability to connect physical, mathematical, and engineering concepts in an interdisciplinary context, fostering independent judgment in the interpretation of results and in the critical assessment of biomedical applications. Students will be encouraged to communicate concepts, methods, and results clearly and rigorously, and to acquire tools for the autonomous study of advanced and rapidly evolving topics in biomedical electromagnetism.

## Prerequisites

The course requires basic knowledge acquired in Mathematics 2, Physics, Geometry, and Algebra, particularly on derivatives, integrals, electromagnetic phenomena, and the use of vectors, which are essential for understanding the behavior of fields in biological tissues.

## Teaching methods

The course includes classroom lectures, possibly supported by audiovisual material, numerical exercises, individual and group design activities, and numerical laboratory sessions. The course also includes flipped classroom activities, organized as student-led seminars on selected topics agreed upon with the instructor. These activities are intended to promote active participation, autonomous study, and critical discussion of the course topics. Students with valid certifications for disabilities or special educational needs are invited, at the beginning of the course, to contact the instructor and/or the degree programme or department representative for disabilities and special educational needs, in order to agree on specific teaching arrangements consistent with the learning objectives of the course.

## Assessment methods

Assessment includes an oral examination consisting of one question on topics that may cover the entire course syllabus. The oral examination accounts for 25% of the final grade. The remaining 75% is based on the evaluation of the activities carried out during the course, divided into three components, each contributing 25% to the final grade: electromagnetic simulation activities, documented through an individual report; numerical exercises, documented through a workbook developed in Jupyter environment; and flipped classroom seminar activities. The assessment criteria include understanding of the physical and engineering principles covered in the course; the ability to apply models and methods to specific problems; the correctness of numerical analyses and simulations; the quality, clarity, and completeness of the submitted documentation; rigor in the interpretation of results; the ability to critically connect the different topics of the course; and the effectiveness of communication during the seminar activities.
