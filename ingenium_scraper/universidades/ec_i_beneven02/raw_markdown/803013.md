## Contents

INTRODUCTION TO THE CIRCUIT MODEL Electric current intensity. Voltage and potential difference. Circuit model: bipoles, Kirchhoff's laws. Power and electrical energy, conventions. Main bipoles: resistor, capacitor, inductor, switch, open and short circuit, diodes, ideal and real voltage and current generators. Considerations on the characteristic of a bipole and on the maximum power supplied. Outline of application limits of the circuit model. PROPERTY OF THE CIRCUIT MODEL - METHODS OF ANALYSIS – STEADY-STATE CIRCUITS Elements of graph theory. Eq. of Kirchhoff in matrix form, eq. of Kirchhoff linearly independent (dim). Canonical form of the equations. Methods of solution of the circuits through the node potentials and the mesh currents. Conservation of electric powers and Tellegen theorem (dim). Non-amplification properties for resistive circuits (dim). Properties of linear resistive circuits. SINUSOIDAL CIRCUITS Sinusoidal circuits, phasors, symbolic or phasors method. Instantaneous and average powers in sinusoidal regime; complex, active, reactive, apparent powers and conservation properties. Boucherot theorem. Examples. ANALYSIS AND SYNTHESIS OF DOUBLE BIPOLES Circuit elements with multiple terminals (N-poles), double bipoles. Double resistive bipoles and matrix representations (G, R, H and T). I, II and III form of reciprocity of the resistive d.b. (dim.). Controlled generators. Analysis and synthesis of double bipoles. DYNAMIC CIRCUITS OF THE I AND II ORDER Dynamics of RC and RL circuits of the first order. Transient and permanent regime. Free and forced evolution. Dissipative circuits. Dynamics of second order circuits: RLC series. Continuity of state variables. Formulation with state equations. Associated resistive circuit method.

## Learning objectives

The course aims to provide students with the methodological tools required for the modelling, analysis, and synthesis of electrical systems through the theory of electrical networks and circuits, highlighting its role as a general paradigm for the modelling of physical systems in engineering. The course introduces the fundamental methodologies for the analysis and synthesis of electrical circuits under steady-state, sinusoidal, and dynamic operating conditions, providing the theoretical foundations necessary for the study of electrical signals and the operating principles of electrical equipment and systems. Upon successful completion of the course, students will have achieved the following learning outcomes. D1. Knowledge and Understanding Students will acquire a thorough understanding of the fundamental principles of electromagnetism and electrical circuit theory. They will be able to understand the circuit model, the fundamental laws governing electrical networks, the behaviour of the main electrical components, and the methodologies for analysing circuits under DC, sinusoidal steady-state, and dynamic operating conditions. Furthermore, they will understand the role of electrical network theory as a general modelling framework for physical systems, recognizing its applicability across different engineering disciplines. D2. Applying Knowledge and Understanding Students will be able to apply the acquired knowledge to analyse, solve, and synthesize simple electrical circuits by selecting the most appropriate modelling approach and analysis methodology. They will also be able to develop design solutions consistent with given specifications, critically evaluating their performance, complexity, and effectiveness. D3. Making Judgements Students will develop the ability to critically evaluate different circuit configurations, identifying the best compromise between simplicity, effectiveness, and design requirements. They will also be able to interpret the behaviour of electrical circuits based on the physical laws governing their operation. D4. Communication Skills Students will be able to communicate the fundamental principles of electrical engineering using appropriate technical language, correctly employ the terminology of the discipline, represent electrical circuits using standard electrical engineering methods and conventions, and rigorously discuss the analytical procedures adopted. D5. Learning Skills Students will develop a study approach based on the mathematical modelling of electrical systems and electrical network theory, acquiring competencies that can be transferred to advanced courses in electrical, energy, and automation engineering. They will also be able to identify analogies among physical systems belonging to different engineering fields by using electrical network theory as a general framework for analysis and modelling.

## Teaching methods

The course consists of 48 hours of teaching activities, including lectures and guided problem-solving sessions. In accordance with the overall workload assigned to the course, the teaching activities are approximately organized as follows: • 36 hours (approximately 75%) of lectures devoted to the theoretical and methodological foundations of Electrical Engineering, with particular emphasis on electrical circuit theory, electrical network analysis, and the fundamentals of electromagnetism. • 12 hours (approximately 25%) of guided problem-solving sessions, during which students address engineering problems of progressively increasing complexity in order to consolidate the theoretical concepts introduced during the lectures and develop the ability to analyse and synthesize electrical circuits. Lectures are delivered using either traditional blackboard teaching or real-time handwritten notes on a tablet projected in the classroom. This teaching approach promotes the progressive development of theoretical concepts and encourages active student participation. The lecture notes produced during each class are made available to students in PDF format immediately after the lecture. Additional teaching materials, solved exercises, and course announcements are provided through the University's HANDY e-learning platform, supporting students' independent study throughout the semester. Attendance is not compulsory, although it is strongly recommended, particularly for the guided problem-solving sessions, where students develop the analytical and methodological skills that constitute an integral part of the course learning outcomes. Working students and students who are unable to attend classes regularly may access all teaching materials available on the HANDY platform and are encouraged to contact the instructor to discuss appropriate arrangements supporting their individual study. Students with Specific Learning Disorders (SLD), disabilities, or other special educational needs are encouraged to contact both the instructor and the University's Disability and SLD Coordinator at the beginning of the course in order to discuss any appropriate teaching accommodations. Such arrangements, while fully respecting the intended learning outcomes of the course, may include adaptations concerning study organization, access to teaching materials, and participation in teaching activities, in accordance with current legislation and the University's guidelines.

## Assessment methods

The assessment is designed to verify the achievement of the intended learning outcomes of the course, with particular emphasis on students' understanding of the theoretical foundations of Electrical Engineering, their ability to analyse and synthesize electrical circuits, their ability to solve engineering problems independently, and their command of the technical language of the discipline. The final examination consists of a compulsory written examination followed by a compulsory oral examination. a) Written examination (2 hours). The written examination consists of two compulsory problems covering steady-state, sinusoidal steady-state, and first- and second-order dynamic circuits, together with one or more optional problems on the remaining course topics. Assessment is based on the correct formulation of the circuit model, the selection of the appropriate analysis method, the correctness of the mathematical development, the critical interpretation of the results, and the clarity of the graphical and symbolic representations. b) Oral examination. The oral examination normally covers two or three topics from the course syllabus through definitions, demonstrations (where applicable), and practical examples. Students awarded a D grade in the written examination begin the oral examination with an additional circuit analysis problem. The oral examination assesses students' knowledge of the course topics, understanding of the physical and mathematical principles of Electrical Engineering, ability to establish connections among different topics, command of the technical terminology, and ability to discuss theoretical demonstrations and engineering applications. The written examination is graded according to the following symbolic scale: A (28–30/30), B (25–27/30), C (21–24/30), D (18–20/30), and INS (fail, not admitted to the oral examination). Results are published within seven days. Passing the written examination is required for admission to the oral examination and is valid only for the immediately following oral session (normally within 5–10 days). The final grade, expressed on a 30-point scale, is awarded after the oral examination and is not the arithmetic average of the two examinations. Instead, it is based on the student's overall performance, considering the written examination, theoretical understanding, ability to integrate different course topics, and scientific maturity in problem analysis. As a guideline, the written and oral examinations contribute approximately 33% and 67%, respectively, to the final grade. For attending students, two mid-term examinations are offered as an alternative to the final written examination. The first (late October–early November) focuses mainly on steady-state circuits, while the second (late November–early December) covers sinusoidal steady-state circuits, dynamic circuits, and two-port networks. The mid-term examinations follow the same assessment criteria as the final written examination. Students who successfully pass both mid-term examinations are admitted directly to the oral examination before the end of the semester. Students with Specific Learning Disorders (SLD), disabilities, or other special educational needs are encouraged to contact the instructor and the University's Disability and SLD Coordinator at the beginning of the course to arrange appropriate examination accommodations, including compensatory measures and reasonable adjustments, in accordance with current legislation, the University's guidelines, and the intended learning outcomes of the course.
