## Contents

1) Introduction to system modelling (fundamentals) Concept of a model: purpose, level of detail, accuracy/complexity trade-off. System, subsystem, boundaries, state variables, inputs, outputs, disturbances. System classification: SISO/MIMO, LTI/nonlinear, continuous-time/discrete-time, deterministic/stochastic (overview). 2) Physical lumped-parameter modelling and multi-domain analogies Balances and constitutive laws: conservation principles and dynamics. Typical structures: storage, dissipation, conversion elements. Mathematical analogies across domains (e.g., electrical–mechanical–thermal–fluidic). Cross-domain examples (depending on the degree programme): RLC networks and circuit models (electronics). Mass–spring–damper models (mechanics). RC thermal models (thermal systems). Tank and pipe models (fluid systems). 3) Mathematical representations of dynamic systems Ordinary differential equations (ODEs) and nonlinear models. Input–state–output form: definition and physical interpretation of states. Relationship with the transfer function for LTI systems. Subsystem interconnections: series/parallel/feedback, modular modelling. 4) Identification and validation (operational introduction) Why identification is needed: uncertain parameters and data-informed models. Parameter fitting from experimental data: least squares (basics), model selection, model/data comparison. Quality metrics and validation (error, residuals, overfitting: essential concepts). 5) Numerical simulation ODE integration methods: Euler, Runge–Kutta (concepts), adaptive solvers. Numerical stability, local/global error, step-size choice. Stiff systems: recognition and impact on simulation (overview). Discretisation and discrete-time simulation: sampling, aliasing (essential reminders). 6) Modelling and simulation in Matlab/Simulink (guided lab) Model implementation in Matlab: scripts, functions, simulation and post-processing. Simulink: block-based modelling, solver settings, parameters, signal logging. Parametric analyses, scenarios, parameter sweeps, comparison of modelling architectures. Good practices: traceability, units, lightweight model versioning, simulation reporting. 7) Case studies focused on electronics and biomedicine

## Learning objectives

The course introduces the principles and methods of modelling and simulation of natural and artificial dynamical systems, with particular attention to applications in the electronic and biomedical fields. The general objective is to provide the student with the concept of abstract system and with the whole methodological workflow leading from physical reality to the model: modelling, parameter identification, validation, numerical simulation, analysis and use of the model for design, monitoring and control. D1. Knowledge and understanding At the end of the course the student will be able to: • describe the concept of dynamical system and its mathematical representations (input–state–output and, for LTI systems, transfer function); • understand the balance equations and constitutive laws underlying lumped-parameter physical modelling across different domains (mechanical, thermal, fluidic, electrical/electronic); • recognise the analogies between physical domains and multi-domain interactions; • understand the fundamentals of numerical methods for the integration of differential equations and the concepts of stability, accuracy and stiffness. D2. Applying knowledge and understanding The student will be able to apply: • procedures for deriving lumped-parameter models starting from a real physical system; • parameter identification techniques on experimental data (least squares) and model validation; • numerical simulation methods in the Matlab/Simulink environment, with an appropriate choice of solvers and integration steps; • parametric analyses and comparison between simulated results and experimental data. D3. Making judgements The student will be able to assess and interpret: • the adequacy of a model with respect to its purpose, managing the trade-off between accuracy and complexity; • the quality of an identified model through the analysis of errors, residuals and overfitting phenomena; • the reliability of simulation results in relation to numerical stability and error. D4. Communication skills The student will be able to communicate: • the modelling choices and their results through clear and reproducible technical reports; • the interpretation of simulation results with appropriate use of terminology, units of measurement and graphical representations. D5. Learning skills The student will develop the ability to: • autonomously extend the acquired methodologies to new systems and application domains; • consult technical and scientific documentation (including the official MathWorks documentation) to deepen tools and methods; • independently tackle modelling and simulation problems in professional contexts or in subsequent studies.

## Prerequisites

The course of Automatic Control is a pre-requisite for this course. Indeed, for a profitable attendance, a basic knowledge of calculus (differential and integral calculus, ordinary differential equations), linear algebra (matrices, eigenvalues) and fundamentals of physics is recommended. A minimal familiarity with the Matlab computing environment is useful.

## Teaching methods

Consistently with the overall workload of the course (48 hours, 6 CFU), the teaching activity is indicatively distributed as follows: • Lectures: about 32 hours (≈ 67%); • Tutorials/exercises, also with the aid of computer tools: about 10 hours (≈ 21%); • Guided laboratory activities in the Matlab/Simulink environment: about 6 hours (≈ 12%). The course does not include mandatory attendance, but participation in the teaching activities is strongly recommended. Teaching materials and guidance for individual study are available on the University e-learning platform Handy, accessible also to working and non-attending students. ## Assessment methods

Learning is assessed through a final examination aimed at verifying the achievement of the expected learning outcomes (D1–D5). The examination includes applied exercises (modelling, analysis and/or simulation) and theory questions, and is complemented by the discussion of a report/activity carried out in the Matlab/Simulink environment. The assessment, expressed out of thirty, takes into account: correctness and completeness in solving the exercises; command of the theoretical fundamentals and appropriateness of technical language; ability to justify modelling choices and to critically interpret simulation results. The examination is passed with a minimum mark of 18/30; honours (lode) are awarded in case of full command, autonomy of judgement and particular clarity of exposition.
