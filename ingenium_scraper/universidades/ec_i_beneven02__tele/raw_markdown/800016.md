## Contents

The course program includes the treatment of the following topics: Introduction to communication systems, with particular reference to aspects involving probability and random variables (source, source coding, Gaussian channel) and signal representation (modulations). Foundations of probability and random variables: Axiomatic approach. Definition of experiment. Sample space. Event space. Probability measure. Kolmogorov axioms. Continuous and discrete probability spaces. Independence. Conditional probability. Total probability theorem. Bayes’ law. Random variables. CDF and PDF of a random variable. Properties of CDF and PDF. Uniform, exponential, Poisson, Bernoulli, and Gaussian CDF and PDF. Q-function. Moments of a random variable. Calculation of probabilities associated with Gaussian random variables. Pairs of random variables, CDF, pmf, and pdf. Joint moments, covariance and correlation, covariance and correlation matrices. Pairs of Gaussian variables. Introduction to random processes, complete and synthetic characterization. Statistical autocorrelation function. Strict-sense and wide-sense stationarity. Signals and systems in the time domain: Elementary deterministic continuous-time and discrete-time signals. Operations on signals. Time average, power, and energy. Signal space. Correlation functions. LTI systems. System classification (linearity, dispersiveness, causality, stationarity, stability). Superposition principle. Impulse response and convolution integral. Input-output relationships for correlation functions. Signals and systems in the frequency domain: Representation of continuous and discrete signals in the frequency domain. Fourier transform and its properties. Frequency response. Parseval’s equality. Replication and sampling properties. Energy and power spectral density. Wiener-Khinchin theorem. Input-output relationships for energy and power spectral densities. Signal sampling: Ideal sampling and reconstruction formula.

## Learning objectives

The general objective is to provide students with the fundamental concepts and methodologies for the analysis of random variables and signals, which form the basis of communication systems. D1. Knowledge and understanding Upon completion of the course, the student will be able to: understand the fundamental theoretical concepts related to probability theory and deterministic and random signals; assimilate the approach to probability and the properties of random variables (including notable distributions and the Q-function); deeply understand the representation and analysis of signals and LTI systems in both the time and frequency domains. D2. Applying knowledge and understanding The student will be able to: calculate probabilities associated with random variables in general and, in particular, with Gaussian variables; apply the studied mathematical models to analyze, classify, and determine the time and frequency responses of linear time-invariant systems, with potential applications to communication systems; determine averages, energies, powers, and spectral densities; utilize ideal sampling and reconstruction formulas. D3. Making judgments The student will be able to evaluate/interpret: the effectiveness and performance of an elementary system in the presence of bandwidth limitations or Gaussian noise; select the most appropriate probabilistic calculation models and spectral analysis methodologies to describe specific phenomena; independently interpret numerical results deriving from theoretical exercises. D4. Communication skills The student will be able to communicate: the problems, technical solutions, and engineering properties of signals using a rigorous and appropriate technical language; clearly, coherently, and concisely present the analysis methodologies applied within the exams (both written and oral), scientifically arguing the connections between the various domains of signal representation. D5. Learning skills The student will develop the capacity to: independently deepen more advanced topics within telecommunications engineering; consult and understand specialized reference textbooks, university lecture notes, and technical documentation of the sector; more easily assimilate subsequent courses in the curriculum that rely on digital technologies and signal processing.

## Assessment methods

The final exam consists of a written test and an oral interview. *Written test composed of: - Two theoretical questions: one related to probability and random variables, and the other related to signals and systems in the time and frequency domains, including signal sampling. - Two numerical exercises: one related to probability and random variables, and the other related to signals and systems in the time and frequency domains, including signal sampling. Each question is graded out of thirty, and a passing grade corresponds to a score of no less than 18. A necessary condition to pass the written test is achieving a passing grade on both theoretical questions and on at least one numerical exercise. To pass the written test and access the oral exam, an overall sufficient average score of no less than 18 must be achieved. *Oral interview: consists of an in-depth discussion of the topics covered in the written test, without adding questions on topics not strictly related to the theoretical and numerical queries of the written exam itself. The interview will be held within one week from the written test. The final evaluation is expressed with a grade out of thirty. Mid-term exams (Partial tests): Attending students have the option (non-mandatory) to split the exam into two partial tests, each consisting of a written test and a respective oral interview. The first test takes place in the middle of the semester and covers probability, random variables, and random signals; the second test takes place at the end of the course and covers signals and systems in the time and frequency domains, including signal sampling. The tests are structured into theoretical and numerical questions, identical to the full exam, and evaluated according to the aforementioned criteria. The partial tests are not valid individually but only if the evaluation is sufficient for both. Students with valid certifications for SLD/DSA, disabilities, or other educational needs are invited to contact the professor or the disability coordinator of the Course/Department before the assessment, in order to agree on any verification methods that, while respecting the course objectives, take into account individual learning needs.
