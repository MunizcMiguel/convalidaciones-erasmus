## Contents

The course syllabus, consistent with the teaching load of 9 ECTS credits and 72 hours, progressively covers the following topics. Part 1 - Combinational networks and finite-state machines: • Boolean algebra and representation of logic functions; • logic gates and basic digital circuits; • analysis and synthesis of combinational machines; • introduction to sequential networks; • synthesis of finite-state machines. Part 2 - Computer structure and design: • computers: abstractions, technology, and levels of organization; • instructions and the language of computers; • data representation and computer arithmetic; • structure and operation of the processor; • datapath and control; • memory hierarchy, cache, and main memory; • mass storage devices and other peripherals; • elements of RISC-V assembly programming and the relationship among instructions, processor, and memory.

## Learning objectives

The general objective of the course is to provide students with the fundamental concepts of computer organization and design, with particular attention to the logical principles underlying digital machines, the operation of the main hardware subsystems, and the factors that affect performance. The course is divided into two parts: the first is devoted to combinational networks and finite-state machines; the second is devoted to computer structure and design, with reference to machine language and the RISC-V architecture. D1. Knowledge and understanding. At the end of the course, students know and understand: the fundamentals of Boolean algebra; the operation of logic gates; the criteria for the synthesis of combinational and sequential networks; the instruction execution model; the main components of a processor; computer arithmetic; the memory hierarchy; the role of mass storage devices and peripherals. D2. Applying knowledge and understanding. Students are able to apply the knowledge acquired to design simple combinational networks and finite-state machines, interpret the behaviour of a processor, write and analyse simple RISC-V assembly programs, and evaluate the effects of architectural choices on performance. D3. Making judgements. Students are able to critically evaluate alternative design solutions at the logical and architectural levels, interpret performance measures and indicators, and recognise the trade-offs among cost, complexity, capacity, and speed. D4. Communication skills. Students are able to describe the organization of a computer using appropriate technical language, justify the choices made in the synthesis of simple circuits, and illustrate the operation of instructions and architectural components. D5. Learning skills. Students develop the ability to independently explore topics in computer architecture, consult technical documentation and reference manuals, and connect the course contents with subsequent courses in the computer science and engineering area.

## Prerequisites

In order to take the final examination, students must have previously passed the Programming 1 examination, as required by the prerequisites of the Degree Programme. The following basic knowledge is also required: the Von Neumann model; introductory notions of processors, memory, and I/O devices; C programming; organization of a process memory, with reference to the global data area, stack, and heap.

## Assessment methods

The assessment is aimed at verifying the achievement of the learning objectives and the student's ability to use the concepts of computer organization and design in an informed way. The final examination consists of a RISC-V assembly programming test followed by an oral examination. The programming test assesses the ability to write, read, and comment on simple assembly code fragments and to relate them to the operation of the architecture. The oral examination assesses knowledge of the topics included in the syllabus, the ability to connect the different topics of the course, and command of technical language. The final grade is expressed out of 30. Admission to the oral examination is allowed only after passing the assembly test, for which no numerical grade is assigned. The oral examination covers the entire syllabus. Assessment criteria include: relevance and correctness of the answers; quality of reasoning; ability to apply the methods studied to specific problems; proper use of technical language; ability to connect logical, architectural, and performance-related aspects. Erasmus or incoming international students may agree with the lecturer, before the examination, on any language-support arrangements compatible with the course learning objectives. Students with valid certifications for Specific Learning Disorders (SLD), disabilities, or other educational needs are invited to contact the lecturer and the disability coordinator of the Degree Programme/Department before the assessment, in order to agree on any arrangements that take individual needs into account.
