## Contents

Part I 1. Course presentation. Introduction to digital systems and their classification (2h lecture) 2. Analysis of discrete-time linear time-invariant systems in the time domain (4h lecture) Input-output and state-space models, Fundamental Problem of System’s Theory (F.P.S.T.). Free evolution and modes. 3. Z-transform (4h lecture) Definition and properties, Z-transform of canonical functions, theorems, inverse Z-transform methods. 4. Analysis of discrete-time linear time-invariant systems in the Z domain (6h lecture, 2h exercises) Use of the Z-transform to solve the F.P.S.T. Free and forced evolution of input-output models and state-space models. Transfer function. Open-loop and closed-loop stability. Internal and external stability. Jury criterion. 5. Specifications for digital control systems design (2h lecture) Specifications for transient and steady-state behavior. Admissible region for the closed-loop poles. 6. Digital control systems design (6h lecture, 4h exercises) Output feedback: direct synthesis. Choice of desired closed-loop model and feasibility constraints. Controller design formula. State feedback: controllability. Canonical controllability form. Pole assignment via state feedback. Part II 7. Sampled-data systems (4h lecture) Ideal sampler, zero-order hold. Transfer function of sampled-data systems in input-output form or state-space form. Sampling transform and connection between s plane and z plane. 8. Digital control systems in the presence of disturbances (3h lecture) Steady-state effect of polynomial or sinusoidal disturbances. Harmonic response of discrete-time systems. 9. Root-locus based design and discretization-based design (8h lecture, 4h exercises) Root-locus based design. Discretization of controllers and filters. Forward- and backward-difference methods. Tustin method and its variants. Step response invariance method. Pole-zero correspondence method. Choice of the sampling period. Time-based discretization of PID controllers. 10. State observers and observer-based state-feedback control (3h lecture, 2h exercises) Luenberger observer and observer-based state-feedback control. Separation principle. Disturbance observers. 11. Implementation of digital controllers on Arduino platform (6h laboratory activity) Arduino platform and hardware interfacing with Matlab-Simulink. Implementation of a digital control system via Arduino-Simulink. Case study: DC motor position and speed control.

## Learning objectives

The course aims to present the fundamentals of the study of discrete-time systems and the analysis and design of digital control systems with reference to linear and time-invariant processes. The course comprises theoretical lessons as well as practical exercises in the context of which the design methodologies are simulatively tested through the Matlab-Simulink software and laboratory activities made up using an Arduino microcontroller. In total, the course includes 42 hours of lectures, 12 hours of computer-based exercises and 6 hours of laboratory activities. Dublin descriptors. - Knowledge and understanding Develop knowledge of the formalisms used to describe discrete-time dynamical systems and their structural properties, and of the methodologies for the analysis and design of discrete-time feedback control systems Applying knowledge and understanding Ability to solve original problems in modeling, analysis, and design of open-loop or feedback discrete-time dynamical systems. Making judgements Ability to handle the complexity of a discrete-time or sampled-data dynamical system by abstracting its physical behavior using a mathematical model. Ability to critically and synergistically use various tools for analysis and design of open-loop or feedback discrete-time dynamical systems. - Communication skills: Ability to produce a formal description of the dynamic behavior of a discrete-time system and any control specifications. Ability to clearly express technical concepts inherent in discrete-time dynamical systems and related digital control systems both verbally and through slides and graphs. - Learning skills: Know how to integrate knowledge from various sources in order to gain a deeper understanding of the phenomena taking place in open-loop or controlled discrete-time dynamical systems.

## Prerequisites

Knowledge related to linear algebra (matrices, determinants, eigenvalues) is required in addition to the knowledge derived from the courses related to the analysis and control of continuous-time dynamical systems. To profitably benefit from the computer-based exercises the students need to possess a basic knowledge of the Matlab-Simulink environment. The possibility of passing the exam through a written report is only granted to students who have successfully passed a laboratory inherent to the use of Matlab-Simulink for dynamic simulation.

## Teaching methods

The course includes theoretical lectures (42 h), computer exercises carried out in the classroom or laboratory in the presence of the teacher (12 h), and laboratory activities (6 h). The lectures are normally carried out by showing and commenting slides which are made available to the students. The exercises consist of solving analysis and design tasks and are carried out also by means of computer simulations using the dynamical simulation software "Matlab-Simulink."

## Assessment methods

The exam is passed through three different ways: 1. Mid-term and final written tests. 2. An oral interview. 3. The preparation of a written report and its presentation through slides. As for the mid-term and final written tests, they are held once per academic year and are reserved to students who have attended at least 75% of the class hours in the classroom. The mid-term test is held at half of the semester and it pertains to the contents of the first 30 hours of classes. The final test is held at the end of the semester, after the conclusion of the lectures. In both the written tests, each question (that either consists of an analysis or design exercise, or the discursive description of specific topics) is assigned a specific score, which is clearly specified in the assignment. Both tests are evaluated with an overall score out of thirty, obtained summing the scores obtained in the single questions. Admission to the final test requires a score of at least 18/30 in the mid-term test. The overall score is the average between the scores obtained in the mid-term and final tests, rounded to the nearest larger integer value. As for the oral interview, the first question concerns the resolution of an exercise of analysis or design, whereas the two additional questions involve a discursive illustration of certain arguments from the program. Depending on the quality level of the answer, every question is assigned a score ranging from 0 to 10, and the final grade is determined by the sum of the scores obtained in the individual questions. Both in the written tests and the oral interview, the type of questions/exercises is chosen to verify the actual acquisition of the learning results, in agreement with the guidelines previously exposed. Concerning the written report, this option is only granted to students who have already successfully passed a laboratory inherent to the use of Matlab-Simulink for dynamic simulation. The written report previews that a concrete control problem, proposed by the student and preliminarily discussed with the teacher, is analyzed and solved in its entirety (starting from the mathematical modeling of the process to be controlled, then outlining reasonable specifications, then designing controllers using at least two different methodologies presented in the course and simulating the designed control systems with the software Matlab-Simulink. A critical discussion of a performance comparison between the different approaches, comprising also the inclusion of nonideality effects such as measurement noise, or sensor/actuator dynamics, is also part of the work. The written report is prepared using Microsoft Word (or an equivalent application) with attached the realized Matlab-Simulink files and then exposed to the teacher during a short interview during which the student makes a presentation (prepared using Microsoft Powerpoint or an equivalent application) which summarizes the achieved results, and clarifies doubts formulated by the teacher. While developing the report, the student can interact two times with the teacher to submit doubts or problems encountered and get feedback on how to solve them. Such a structure allows the complete verification of the actual acquisition of the expected learning outcomes. The written report is evaluated with a grade up to twenty, related to the correctness, completeness, and level of depth of the report, and to the autonomy level shown in its development, whereas the final presentation is evaluated with a grade up to ten, related to the quality and effectiveness of the presentation. The final grade is the sum of the scores obtained for the written report and its presentation.
